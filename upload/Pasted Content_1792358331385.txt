- A judicial opinion is a form of legal opinion written by a judge or judicial panel in the course of resolving a legal dispute, providing the decision reached and usually indicating the facts and legal analysis [44]
- Judicial opinions may be released in several stages: bench opinion (rough explanation), slip opinion (not typeset, subject to revision), and final published edition [44]
- The Supreme Court issues slip opinions with disclaimers noting they may contain computer-generated errors and that print versions control over electronic versions [44]
- Types of judicial opinions include: unanimous (all justices agree), majority (more than half agree), plurality (concurring opinion joined by greatest number when no majority), dissenting (disagreement with majority), and per curiam (anonymous collective decision) [44]
- Dissenting opinions do not create binding precedent but may be cited as persuasive authority and sometimes spur changes in the law [44]
- In common law systems, majority opinions become part of case law and can be cited as precedent by later courts [44]
- A study analyzing four million US court decisions from 1800-2000 found that judicial pathbreaks (disruptiveness) have declined over time, indicating increasing constraint by precedent [45]
- This growing inertia in judicial reasoning appears driven by precedent overload (case law volume growing at exponent 1.7 relative to population) and ideological polarization within the judiciary [45]
- Despite overall path dependence, high-authority courts continue to shape legal discourse through top-down interventions [45]
- A court is an institution with authority to adjudicate legal disputes between parties and administer justice in civil, criminal, and administrative matters [46]
- The practical authority given to a court is known as its jurisdiction, describing its power to decide certain kinds of questions [46]
- Case law is law based on precedents (judicial decisions from previous cases) rather than constitutions, statutes, or regulations [47]
- Stare decisis ("let the decision stand") is the principle by which judges are bound to past decisions [47]
- In common law systems, courts follow stare decisis, with lower courts bound by previous decisions of higher courts [47]

Core concepts & context
A court opinion represents the judge's disposition of a case, including the decision rationale and sentencing 900. A judicial opinion is a form of legal opinion written by a judge or judicial panel in the course of resolving a legal dispute, providing the decision reached and usually indicating the facts and legal analysis 44.

Definition and Structure
Court opinions serve as the formal record of a court's decision and reasoning. They typically include the court's determination on the legal questions presented, the factual findings, and the legal reasoning supporting the decision. In common law systems, these opinions become part of case law and can be cited as precedent by later courts 47. The principle of stare decisis ("let the decision stand") is fundamental to this system, as it binds judges to follow past decisions 47.

Judicial opinions may be released in several stages: bench opinion (rough explanation), slip opinion (not typeset, subject to revision), and final published edition 44. The Supreme Court, for example, issues slip opinions with disclaimers noting they may contain computer-generated errors and that print versions control over electronic versions 44.

Types of Judicial Opinions
Several types of judicial opinions exist, each serving different functions in the legal system:

Unanimous opinions: All judges agree on the outcome and reasoning
Majority opinions: More than half of the judges agree on the outcome and reasoning
Plurality opinions: When no majority exists, the opinion joined by the greatest number of judges
Dissenting opinions: Judges disagree with the majority's reasoning or outcome
Per curiam opinions: Anonymous collective decisions issued by the court as an institution rather than individual judges 44
Dissenting opinions, while not creating binding precedent, may be cited as persuasive authority and sometimes spur changes in the law over time 44. This reflects the dynamic nature of the common law system, where even minority views can contribute to legal evolution.

Current Context and Trends
Recent research indicates that judicial pathbreaks (disruptiveness) have declined over time, suggesting increasing constraint by precedent 45. A study analyzing four million US court decisions from 1800-2000 found this growing inertia appears driven by precedent overload (case law volume growing at exponent 1.7 relative to population) and ideological polarization within the judiciary 45. Despite this overall path dependence, high-authority courts continue to shape legal discourse through top-down interventions 45.

The practical authority given to a court is known as its jurisdiction, describing its power to decide certain kinds of questions 46. Courts are institutions with authority to adjudicate legal disputes between parties and administer justice in civil, criminal, and administrative matters 46. This institutional framework provides the context within which judicial opinions are generated and interpreted.

Relationship to Expert Testimony
While court opinions represent judicial decisions, they may also incorporate expert testimony when appropriate. An expert witness is a person whose opinion by virtue of education, training, certification, skills or experience is accepted by the judge as an expert 43. Expert witnesses may deliver "expert evidence" within the area of their expertise 43, and are typically relied on for opinions on severity of injury, degree of sanity, cause of failure, loss of earnings, and care costs 43. In intellectual property cases, experts may be asked to ascertain similarity between music scores, book texts, or circuit boards 43.

The concept of allowing expert witness testimony was first introduced by Lord Mansfield in the case of Folkes v. Chadd in 1782 43. Expert witnesses are qualified by the court and must be re-qualified each time they come to trial 43, and they are obligated to study processes prior to making a survey or postpone assignment if they lack understanding of specific conditions 43.

Current state & evidence
The current state of court opinions reveals both traditional practices and emerging technological developments. Legal opinions are written explanations by judges or groups of judges that accompany orders or rulings, laying out rationale and legal principles 15. In some jurisdictions, the term "legal opinion" may refer to written advice from lawyers rather than judges, creating potential ambiguity in terminology 15.

Types and Publication of Judicial Opinions
Judicial opinions typically fall into several categories, including majority opinions, dissenting opinions, and plurality opinions 33. These opinions are generally published when courts interpret law in novel ways or handle high-profile cases of public interest 15. However, not all cases result in published opinions; many receive memorandum decisions that don't establish legal precedent 15. The published opinions constitute case law, which serves as a major source of law in common law systems 15.

Judicial opinions play a crucial role in legal systems by reinforcing, changing, establishing, or overturning legal precedent 15. In contrast, civil law systems (practiced in about 150 countries) rely primarily on legal codes rather than judicial precedent 38. This fundamental difference shapes how court opinions function across different legal traditions.

Technological Developments in Legal Opinion Analysis
Recent technological advances have introduced new approaches to analyzing and generating court opinions. The AgentsCourt framework represents a multi-agent system for judicial decision-making that simulates court debate, legal resource retrieval, and decision-making refinement 13. This framework utilizes SimuCourt, a judicial benchmark with 420 Chinese judgment documents spanning three common case types, and Legal-KB, a large-scale legal knowledge base 13. AgentsCourt has demonstrated significant improvements in generating legal articles, achieving 8.6% and 9.1% F1 score improvements in first and second instance settings 13.

Another development is CuRIAM, an annotation schema for categorizing types of legal metalanguage in U.S. Supreme Court opinions 14. Metalanguage refers to language used to comment on or draw attention to other legal texts 14. CuRIAM has been applied to a corpus of 59k tokens from U.S. Supreme Court opinions 14, enabling more sophisticated analysis of how judges frame their legal reasoning.

Foreign Legal Opinions and Commercial Applications
In commercial contexts, foreign legal opinions serve specialized functions in cross-border transactions 126. These documents have become highly standardized over time, with most following a regimented format 126. Originally stemming from the doctrine of ultra vires (ensuring foreign corporations had capacity to enter transactions), these opinions have expanded to cover various aspects including exchange controls, usury laws, licensing requirements, tax consequences, and judgment enforcement 126.

Banks increasingly required foreign legal opinions during the 1970s and 1980s to ensure legal validity and enforceability of loans 126. These requirements influenced Basel capital adequacy standards for banks, resulting in more favorable capital weighting for loans supported by opinions 126. Foreign legal opinions typically confirm jurisdiction, list assumptions, and set out opinion statements without providing detailed reasoning 126, though in areas of legal uncertainty, they may be supplemented with a second, reasoned opinion 126.
